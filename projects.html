Project 01

Jev Decision Engine: fast, explainable AI decisions for everyday choices

What I built

I built a two-model decision system. Jev, a small, fast decision model from TypeSafe, reads a short question and some context and answers yes/no, pick-one, or rating questions with a calibrated probability in under a second, for a fraction of a cent. Claude sits on top: it frames the questions, checks the answers Jev is least sure about, and explains the results in plain language. The two are wired together with an MCP server, a Claude skill, and a Google Apps Script automation that scores new rows in a tracking spreadsheet every 15 minutes.

What is Jev

Jev is a decision model built by TypeSafe and reached through OpenRouter's Decisions API. Instead of writing a paragraph, it reads a short prompt and a question and returns a probability, which makes it fast and cheap enough to run on every item in a list rather than just one.

Why it matters

Use the smallest model that does the job well. A single yes/no or pick-one question doesn't need a large, expensive model — it needs a fast, calibrated one.

Why it matters

Every answer comes with a confidence level, so a person only has to review the cases Jev is unsure about — the same pattern used in fraud and risk decisioning.

Why it matters

Tested against my own real outcome data: asking the right question mattered more than which model answered it, and about 1,000 decisions cost roughly nine cents.

What the results tell us

  • The loudest email ranked dead last. Urgency is about the sender's deadline; importance is about your goals.
  • The quietest email ranked near the top: the manager's no-subject note is about Friday's renewal and says "concerns." Jev looked at who sent it and what it touches, not how it looks.
  • Jev says when it isn't sure: it's certain the CFO email is critical but unsure it needs action today, because it's due Tuesday noon. A person would say "start today" anyway — exactly where human judgment adds value.
  • The real to-do list is 4 emails, not 10: three decisions and a conversation with the manager, one quick hand-off, and five that can wait or be ignored.

"Which of these text messages are scams?"

Anyone with a phone gets these. It connects to my field — fraud and identity — and shows how fraud professionals look at behavior and what a message asks you to do, not how it looks.

Showing results captured Sep 26, 2026.

What the results tell us

  • The most official-looking message is a scam. Real banks don't send a link and a countdown. Scams share one recipe: urgency plus a link or payment plus a threat or prize. The two safe messages contain none of it.
  • The same message can be safe or dangerous depending on context: a verification code is safe only if you just requested it. If one arrives when you didn't log in, someone else is trying to get into your account. Jev hedges here because context is everything.
  • "Verify first" is the default even for real messages: the cost of checking is seconds, the cost of being wrong once can be your savings. That's how fraud teams think.
  • The "Hi Mom" scam works on emotion, not technology. The fix is just as simple: call the old number.

"Approve, step up, or decline this new account?"

The same technique inside my own field — identity and fraud risk decisioning. Jev picks one of three actions, the way real account-opening systems route applicants, and says how sure it is.

Showing results captured Sep 26, 2026.

What the results tell us

  • Fraud isn't one red flag, it's a pattern. Applicant 2 has no single smoking gun, but everything is new, the device is shared with 7 other "people," and the SSN is too new for someone claiming to be 41. Together that's the fingerprint of a synthetic identity — a person who doesn't exist, built from real and fake data.
  • Good customers have history: Applicant 1 is approved because years of consistent history line up across email, phone, device, credit, and address.
  • Most real decisions are in the middle: Applicant 3 is mostly clean with one mismatch, so Jev routes to a quick extra check instead of guessing. Good systems add a little friction where there's doubt, instead of rejecting a real customer or waving through a fraudster.

Project 02

Visitor Device Intelligence: what a fraud-grade signal stack sees about your visit

What I built

This site already runs a device-intelligence and analytics stack in the background: Fingerprint for device and bot detection, Microsoft Clarity for session replay, and Google Analytics for traffic. This panel exposes one slice of it, live: click the button and a small serverless function calls the same identity signal already active on this page, adds network context from Cloudflare's edge, and shows you the read-out about your own current visit — the way a bank or lender's risk system would see it.

Why it matters

These are the actual signal categories used in identity and fraud risk decisioning at scale — my domain for 18 years — demonstrated on one real visit instead of a synthetic one.

Why it matters

Nothing here is hidden: this is the same analytics already disclosed in the site's privacy note, just shown to you instead of only to me.

Why it matters

No decision gets made from this. It's a read-out, not a score used to grant, deny, or flag anything.
